------------------------------------------------------------
  WHY THIS UNIT MATTERS
------------------------------------------------------------

  . Building a simulation model is only HALF the work.
  . The other half is making sure:
    . The model actually represents reality (Validation)
    . The model is built correctly without bugs (Verification)
    . The experiment is designed properly (Experiment Design)
    . The output is interpreted correctly (Output Analysis)

  . A beautifully coded simulation that models the WRONG
    thing is completely useless.

  . This unit answers three fundamental questions:
    . Is our model CORRECT?     → Verification
    . Is our model REALISTIC?   → Validation
    . Do our results MEAN anything? → Output Analysis

  . KEY DISTINCTION (memorize this):
    .......................................................
    VERIFICATION = "Did we BUILD the model RIGHT?"
                   (Is the code doing what we intended?)

    VALIDATION   = "Did we build the RIGHT model?"
                   (Does the model match the real system?)
    .......................................................

  . Scenario to remember this:
    A programmer simulates a hospital queue.
    The code runs perfectly — no bugs. (Verified)
    But she assumed patients arrive every 5 minutes
    when in reality they arrive every 2 minutes.
    The model is VERIFIED but NOT VALIDATED.
    Outputs will be completely wrong. ✗

7.1  CALIBRATION AND VALIDATION OF MODELS
------------------------------------------------------------

  . WHAT IS VALIDATION?
  .....................

    . The process of confirming that the simulation model
      accurately represents the REAL WORLD system it
      is supposed to mimic.

    . Think of it as: does our model behave the way
      the actual system behaves?

    . Validation is an ongoing process — not a one-time check.

  . WHAT IS CALIBRATION?
  .....................

    . Adjusting the model's parameters until its output
      closely matches observed real-world data.

    . Like tuning a musical instrument — you adjust until
      the sound (output) matches what it should be.

    . Scenario:
      A traffic simulation shows average speed = 45 km/h.
      Real traffic data shows average speed = 38 km/h.
      You adjust vehicle density, signal timing parameters
      until the simulation also shows ~38 km/h.
      That process of adjustment = CALIBRATION.


  ....................................
  A. FACE VALIDATION
  ....................................

    . The SIMPLEST and FIRST validation method used.
    . Show the model and its outputs to DOMAIN EXPERTS
      (people who know the real system well).
    . Ask: "Does this look right to you?"
    . If experts say the behavior looks realistic
      → model passes face validation.

    . Also called: Subjective Validation or Expert Review.

    . WHAT IS CHECKED:
      . Does the model structure make sense?
      . Do the outputs follow expected trends?
      . Do extreme inputs produce sensible extreme outputs?
      . Does the model behavior match intuition?

    . SCENARIO:
      A hospital queue simulation is built.
      The hospital administrator (domain expert) reviews it.
      She checks:
        . When 50 patients arrive per hour and
          only 2 doctors are available, does the
          waiting time go up sharply? → YES ✓
        . When a third doctor is added, does
          waiting time drop significantly? → YES ✓
        . Does adding more beds reduce ward wait? → YES ✓
      She says: "This behaves like our real hospital."
      → FACE VALIDATION PASSED ✓

    . EXAMPLE WITH DATA:
      Bank simulation output (face validation check):

      Scenario           Expected (Real Bank)  Simulation Output
      ...............................................................
      1 teller, 20/hr    Long queue, 15 min    Queue=18, wait=14 min
      2 tellers, 20/hr   Short queue, 4 min    Queue=3, wait=4.5 min
      3 tellers, 30/hr   Moderate, 6 min       Queue=5, wait=5.8 min
      3 tellers, 5/hr    Very short, <1 min    Queue=0, wait=0.8 min

      All trends match expected direction → Face Validation ✓

    . ADVANTAGE:
      . Quick, cheap, no formal math needed.
      . Catches obvious structural errors early.

    . LIMITATION:
      . Subjective — depends on expert's opinion.
      . Cannot catch subtle statistical errors.
      . Expert may not notice small discrepancies.


  ....................................
  B. VALIDATION FOR ASSUMPTIONS
  ....................................

    . Every simulation model is built on a set of ASSUMPTIONS.
    . These assumptions must be checked against reality.
    . If a core assumption is wrong, ALL results are wrong.

    . COMMON ASSUMPTIONS IN SIMULATION:
      . Arrival rate follows Poisson distribution.
      . Service time follows Exponential distribution.
      . Queue is FIFO (first come, first served).
      . System is in steady state.
      . Customers are independent (one does not affect another).

    . HOW TO VALIDATE ASSUMPTIONS:
      1. Collect real data from the actual system.
      2. Test whether the data fits the assumed distribution.
      3. Use statistical tests (chi-square, K-S test).

    . SCENARIO AND DATA EXAMPLE:
      Assumption: "Customers arrive at the bank counter
      following a Poisson distribution with lambda = 10/hour."

      We collect REAL data for 10 one-hour periods:
      Hour     Actual Arrivals    Expected (Poisson, λ=10)
      .....................................................
      1        9                  10
      2        11                 10
      3        10                 10
      4        12                 10
      5        8                  10
      6        10                 10
      7        11                 10
      8        9                  10
      9        10                 10
      10       10                 10

      The real arrivals are close to expected Poisson values.
      Chi-square test confirms Poisson fits the data.
      → Assumption is VALIDATED ✓

    . ANOTHER EXAMPLE — WRONG ASSUMPTION CAUGHT:
      Assumption: "Service time = 3 minutes exactly (constant)."
      Real data collected:
        2.1, 4.5, 1.8, 6.2, 3.1, 2.8, 5.0, 1.5, 3.7, 4.0 minutes
      Mean = 3.47, Std Dev = 1.42

      Service times are NOT constant — they follow an
      Exponential distribution with mean 3.47 minutes.
      Assumption of constant service time is WRONG.
      → Fix the model, use Exponential distribution. ✗→✓


  ....................................
  C. OUTPUT DATA VALIDATION
  ....................................

    . Compare the OUTPUTS of the simulation with
      REAL observed outputs from the actual system.
    . This is the most rigorous form of validation.
    . If simulation output ≈ real system output
      → model is considered valid.

    . METHOD:
      1. Run the simulation for a defined time period.
      2. Collect the same performance measures from
         the real system over the same period.
      3. Compare: are they statistically similar?

    . DATA EXAMPLE:
      Single server bank simulation vs real bank data.
      Period: 8 hours, 80 customers.

      Performance Measure    Real Bank    Simulation    Difference
      ..............................................................
      Avg waiting time       4.2 min      4.5 min       +0.3 min
      Avg service time       3.0 min      3.1 min       +0.1 min
      Max queue length       9 customers  10 customers  +1
      Server utilization     82%          84%           +2%
      Avg time in system     7.2 min      7.6 min       +0.4 min

      All differences are small (< 5%).
      → Simulation output is acceptably close to reality.
      → OUTPUT VALIDATION PASSED ✓

    . WHAT IF OUTPUT DOES NOT MATCH?
      . Re-check input data (arrival rates, service times).
      . Re-check assumptions (queue discipline, priority).
      . Re-calibrate model parameters.
      . Run more simulation replications for stable averages.


  ....................................
  D. VERIFICATION AND VALIDATION OF SIMULATOR MODELS
  ....................................

    . VERIFICATION (checking the MODEL ITSELF):
      . Does the computer code correctly implement
        the conceptual model?
      . Is there a bug in the simulation program?
      . Are the formulas coded correctly?
      . Are random number streams used correctly?

      VERIFICATION TECHNIQUES:
      . Code walkthrough / desk checking
      . Use simple, known inputs and check if output is correct
      . Test extreme values (what if arrival rate = 0?)
      . Use structured programming and modular testing
      . Compare sub-model outputs with hand calculations

      SCENARIO:
        Queue simulation: manually calculate by hand
        the expected waiting time for 5 customers arriving
        at specific times with known service durations.
        Compare hand result with simulation result.
        If they match → code is verified ✓

    . COMBINED V&V APPROACH:
      . First VERIFY (make sure code is correct).
      . Then VALIDATE (make sure model is realistic).
      . Both are necessary — neither alone is sufficient.

      .......................................................
      Status                Result
      .......................................................
      Verified + Validated  GOOD — use the model ✓
      Verified, NOT Valid   Model structure is wrong ✗
      NOT Verified, Valid   Lucky coincidence — fix bugs ✗
      NOT Verified, NOT Valid  Start over ✗✗
      .......................................................

7.2  INPUT VALIDATION USING TURING TEST
------------------------------------------------------------

  . WHAT IS THE TURING TEST IN SIMULATION?
  .........................................

    . Originally proposed by Alan Turing (1950) for AI:
      "Can a machine produce output indistinguishable
       from a human?"

    . In SIMULATION context:
      "Can a domain expert tell the difference between
       REAL SYSTEM output and SIMULATED output?"

    . If the expert CANNOT distinguish them
      → simulation is realistic enough → VALIDATED ✓

    . Also called: Behavioral Validation or
      Operational Validation.


  . HOW IT WORKS:
  ..............

    Step 1: Collect real output data from the actual system.
    Step 2: Collect simulated output from the model.
    Step 3: Mix them together (expert does not know which is which).
    Step 4: Ask the domain expert to identify which is real
            and which is simulated.
    Step 5: If expert cannot tell them apart (or guesses wrong)
            → model output is realistic → VALIDATED.


  . SCENARIO — HOSPITAL QUEUE:
  ............................

    Real hospital waiting room data (Monday, 10 patients):
    Patient  Arrival  Service Start  Wait Time
    .............................................
    1        09:00    09:00          0 min
    2        09:07    09:10          3 min
    3        09:15    09:18          3 min
    4        09:20    09:25          5 min
    5        09:28    09:33          5 min

    Simulation output (same scenario, simulated):
    Patient  Arrival  Service Start  Wait Time
    .............................................
    1        09:00    09:00          0 min
    2        09:06    09:11          5 min
    3        09:14    09:19          5 min
    4        09:21    09:26          5 min
    5        09:29    09:34          5 min

    Both show similar patterns: first patient waits 0,
    queue builds gradually, waiting times stabilize.

    Hospital administrator reviews both datasets
    without labels: "Both look like my hospital on a
    typical Monday morning."
    → Cannot distinguish real from simulated.
    → TURING TEST PASSED ✓


  . DATA EXAMPLE — TRAFFIC SIMULATION:
  .....................................

    Three traffic speed datasets shown to a traffic engineer.
    She must identify which is from real road sensors.

    Dataset A (speeds in km/h over 10 time slots):
    45, 42, 38, 35, 30, 28, 32, 40, 44, 46

    Dataset B (simulated):
    43, 44, 37, 36, 29, 27, 33, 41, 43, 45

    Dataset C (simulated):
    50, 50, 50, 50, 50, 50, 50, 50, 50, 50

    Engineer: "C is clearly NOT real — traffic never
    stays perfectly constant. A and B both look real
    to me — I cannot tell which is the simulation."

    Dataset A was real. Dataset B was simulation.
    Dataset C fails the Turing test badly.
    → Model B passes, Model C fails. ✓ / ✗


  . LIMITATION:
  ............

    . Depends on the expert's experience and perception.
    . Expert may be fooled by a bad model or reject a good one.
    . Should be used alongside statistical validation, not alone.

7.3  DESIGN OF SIMULATION EXPERIMENT WITH
     ELIMINATION OF INITIAL BIAS
------------------------------------------------------------

  . WHAT IS A SIMULATION EXPERIMENT?
  ...................................

    . A systematic plan for running the simulation to
      answer specific questions about the system.
    . Like a scientific experiment — you control inputs
      and observe outputs.

    . Key decisions in experiment design:
      . How long to run the simulation?
      . How many replications (runs) are needed?
      . What starting conditions to use?
      . How to handle initial transient behavior?


  . THE PROBLEM OF INITIAL BIAS (Initialization Bias)
  ...................................................

    . When a simulation STARTS, it begins in an
      ARTIFICIAL initial state (usually empty and idle).
    . Real systems are NEVER completely empty at the
      start of a business day — there are usually
      some customers already present.
    . The early period of simulation does NOT represent
      steady-state behavior.
    . Results from the early period are BIASED.
    . Including them in analysis gives wrong averages.

    . SCENARIO:
      A bank opens at 9 AM. Simulation starts with
      zero customers and zero queue (empty system).
      In reality, by 9:05 AM there are already 8 people
      waiting (early morning rush carried over from
      before opening).

      First 30 minutes of simulation:
        Waiting time = 0.5 minutes (system barely loaded)
      After 30 minutes (steady state):
        Waiting time = 4.2 minutes (system fully loaded)

      If we include the first 30 minutes in our average:
        Overall average = (0.5 + 4.2) / 2 = 2.35 min
        This is WRONG — it understates real congestion.

      If we discard first 30 minutes:
        Average = 4.2 min (true steady-state average) ✓


  . WHAT IS THE WARM-UP PERIOD?
  .............................

    . Also called: Transient Period or Initial Transient.
    . The time needed for the simulation to move from
      its artificial initial state to a realistic
      STEADY STATE that reflects true system behavior.
    . Results collected DURING the warm-up period are
      discarded before analysis begins.

    . How long is the warm-up period?
      . No single formula — depends on the system.
      . Rule of thumb: run until key statistics stabilize.
      . Common methods: Welch's method, graphical inspection.


  . WELCH'S METHOD (Graphical Approach):
  ......................................

    1. Run several replications of the simulation.
    2. Record a key performance measure (e.g., waiting time)
       at each time point.
    3. Average across all replications at each time point.
    4. Plot the average over time.
    5. The point where the plot FLATTENS and becomes
       stable = end of warm-up period.
    6. Discard all data before that point.

    . DATA EXAMPLE:
      10 replications, recording average queue length
      every 10 minutes:

      Time (min)  Rep1  Rep2  Rep3  ... Average
      ............................................
      0           0     0     0         0.0
      10          1     2     1         1.3
      20          3     2     3         2.7
      30          3     4     3         3.3
      40          4     3     4         3.7
      50          4     4     5         4.3
      60          5     4     4         4.3   ← Stabilizes here
      70          4     5     4         4.3
      80          5     4     5         4.7
      90          4     5     5         4.7

      Queue length stabilizes around t=60 minutes.
      → Warm-up period = 60 minutes.
      → Discard first 60 minutes of data from all runs.
      → Use only data from t=60 onwards for analysis. ✓


  . NUMBER OF REPLICATIONS:
  .........................

    . Running the simulation ONCE gives one possible
      outcome (because of randomness).
    . Run multiple replications to get a STABLE AVERAGE.
    . More replications = more reliable estimates.

    . Rule: run enough replications so that the
      confidence interval on your estimate is
      acceptably narrow.

    . DATA EXAMPLE:
      Waiting time estimates across 5 replications:
        Run 1:  4.1 min
        Run 2:  4.8 min
        Run 3:  3.9 min
        Run 4:  5.2 min
        Run 5:  4.0 min
        Mean  = 4.4 min
        Std   = 0.53 min
        95% CI = 4.4 ± 0.47 min  (3.93 to 4.87 min)

      If confidence interval is too wide → run more replications.
      10 replications usually gives more stable results.


  . SUMMARY OF EXPERIMENT DESIGN STEPS:
  ......................................

    Step 1  Define objectives — what question are we answering?
    Step 2  Identify input factors and their ranges
    Step 3  Choose performance measures (output variables)
    Step 4  Determine warm-up period (run pilot, use Welch's)
    Step 5  Decide number of replications needed
    Step 6  Run the simulation experiment
    Step 7  Collect output data (after warm-up period only)
    Step 8  Analyze results

7.4  REGRESSION ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------------------

  . WHAT IS REGRESSION IN SIMULATION?
  ....................................

    . Regression analysis finds the MATHEMATICAL RELATIONSHIP
      between one or more INPUT variables (predictors)
      and an OUTPUT variable (response).

    . In simulation output analysis:
      . INPUT  = simulation parameters we can control
                 (number of servers, arrival rate, service rate)
      . OUTPUT = performance measure we care about
                 (waiting time, queue length, throughput)

    . Regression tells us:
      . WHICH inputs most strongly affect the output.
      . HOW MUCH the output changes per unit change in input.
      . Lets us PREDICT output for new input combinations.

    . Most commonly used: SIMPLE LINEAR REGRESSION and
      MULTIPLE LINEAR REGRESSION.


  ....................................
  A. SIMPLE LINEAR REGRESSION
  ....................................

    . One input variable (X), one output variable (Y).

    . MODEL:  Y = a + b.X + error

      . Y = output (response variable)
      . X = input (predictor variable)
      . a = intercept (Y value when X = 0)
      . b = slope (change in Y per unit change in X)
      . error = random variation unexplained by model

    . FORMULAS:
      b = [n.Σ(XY) - ΣX.ΣY] / [n.ΣX² - (ΣX)²]
      a = (ΣY - b.ΣX) / n

    . SCENARIO:
      We run a bank queue simulation with different
      numbers of tellers (X) and record average
      customer waiting time in minutes (Y).

      DATA TABLE:
      Tellers (X)    Avg Wait Time Y (min)
      ......................................
      1              18.5
      2              8.2
      3              4.6
      4              2.9
      5              1.8

      n = 5
      ΣX  = 1+2+3+4+5 = 15
      ΣY  = 18.5+8.2+4.6+2.9+1.8 = 36.0
      ΣXY = (1×18.5)+(2×8.2)+(3×4.6)+(4×2.9)+(5×1.8)
          = 18.5+16.4+13.8+11.6+9.0 = 69.3
      ΣX² = 1+4+9+16+25 = 55

      b = [5×69.3 - 15×36.0] / [5×55 - 15²]
        = [346.5 - 540] / [275 - 225]
        = -193.5 / 50
        = -3.87

      a = (36.0 - (-3.87)×15) / 5
        = (36.0 + 58.05) / 5
        = 94.05 / 5
        = 18.81

      REGRESSION EQUATION:  Y = 18.81 - 3.87 . X

      INTERPRETATION:
        . When X=0 (no tellers), wait time ≈ 18.81 min (intercept)
        . Each additional teller reduces wait by 3.87 min
        . Predicted wait with 6 tellers:
          Y = 18.81 - 3.87×6 = 18.81 - 23.22 = -4.41 ← impossible!
          (Shows linear model breaks down at extremes)


  ....................................
  B. MULTIPLE LINEAR REGRESSION
  ....................................

    . Multiple input variables (X1, X2, X3...) affecting Y.

    . MODEL:  Y = a + b1.X1 + b2.X2 + b3.X3 + ... + error

    . SCENARIO:
      We run a hospital simulation with two inputs:
        X1 = number of doctors
        X2 = number of nurses
      Output:
        Y = average patient waiting time (minutes)

      SIMULATION RESULTS:
      X1 (Doctors)  X2 (Nurses)  Y (Wait Time min)
      ..............................................
      1             2            24.0
      1             3            20.0
      2             2            14.0
      2             3            11.0
      3             3            7.0
      3             4            5.0
      4             4            3.0

      After fitting multiple regression (using software):
      Y = 35.5 - 5.2.X1 - 3.1.X2

      INTERPRETATION:
        . Each additional doctor reduces wait by 5.2 min.
        . Each additional nurse reduces wait by 3.1 min.
        . Doctors have MORE impact per person than nurses
          in this scenario.
        . Predict wait with 4 doctors and 5 nurses:
          Y = 35.5 - 5.2×4 - 3.1×5
            = 35.5 - 20.8 - 15.5
            = -0.8 ← near zero, model at its boundary


  ....................................
  C. COEFFICIENT OF DETERMINATION — R²
  ....................................

    . R² measures HOW WELL the regression line fits the data.
    . R² ranges from 0 to 1.

    . R² = 0.0  →  X explains NOTHING about Y
    . R² = 0.5  →  X explains 50% of variation in Y
    . R² = 1.0  →  X explains 100% of variation in Y (perfect fit)

    . For the teller example above:
      If R² = 0.95 → 95% of variation in wait time is
      explained by number of tellers. Strong relationship. ✓

    . R² calculation:
      R² = 1 - [Σ(Yi - Ŷi)²] / [Σ(Yi - Ymean)²]
      where Ŷi = predicted value, Ymean = mean of all Y


  ....................................
  D. USING REGRESSION IN SIMULATION ANALYSIS
  ....................................

    . After running a simulation experiment, use regression to:
      . Identify which parameters MOST affect performance.
      . Find the optimal combination of inputs.
      . Build a RESPONSE SURFACE (how output changes
        across the whole range of inputs).
      . Make predictions without running full simulation
        for every possible combination.

    . This is called METAMODELING or SURROGATE MODELING —
      the regression equation acts as a fast approximation
      of the slower simulation.

7.5  ANALYSIS OF VARIANCE — ANOVA
------------------------------------------------------------

  . WHAT IS ANOVA?
  ...............

    . Analysis of Variance.
    . A statistical method to compare the MEANS of
      two or more groups and determine if the differences
      between them are STATISTICALLY SIGNIFICANT
      or just due to random chance (sampling variation).

    . In simulation:
      . We run the simulation under DIFFERENT configurations
        (scenarios, parameter settings).
      . ANOVA tells us: "Is the difference in output
        between these scenarios REAL, or could it just
        be random noise from the simulation?"

    . KEY QUESTION ANOVA ANSWERS:
      "Is at least one group mean significantly different
       from the others?"


  ....................................
  A. CORE CONCEPTS
  ....................................

    . NULL HYPOTHESIS (H0):
      All group means are EQUAL.
      H0: μ1 = μ2 = μ3 = ... = μk
      "There is NO significant difference between scenarios."

    . ALTERNATIVE HYPOTHESIS (H1):
      At least ONE group mean is DIFFERENT.
      "At least one scenario gives significantly different output."

    . VARIANCE:
      ANOVA compares two types of variance:
        . BETWEEN-GROUP variance (due to different scenarios)
        . WITHIN-GROUP variance  (due to random simulation noise)

    . F-STATISTIC:
      F = Between-group variance / Within-group variance

      . Large F → differences between groups are REAL → reject H0
      . Small F → differences are just noise → do not reject H0

    . DECISION RULE:
      If F_calculated > F_critical (from table) → reject H0
      → Groups are significantly different.


  ....................................
  B. ONE-WAY ANOVA — STEP BY STEP
  ....................................

    . ONE-WAY ANOVA: one factor being varied (one independent variable).

    . SCENARIO:
      We simulate a bank with 3 different service strategies:
        Strategy A: 1 server
        Strategy B: 2 servers
        Strategy C: 3 servers

      We run 4 replications (runs) of each strategy
      and record average waiting time (minutes):

      DATA TABLE:
      .......................................................
      Run    Strategy A    Strategy B    Strategy C
             (1 server)    (2 servers)   (3 servers)
      .......................................................
      1      18.2          8.1           4.2
      2      19.5          7.8           4.8
      3      17.8          8.5           3.9
      4      20.1          8.0           4.5
      .......................................................
      Mean   18.9          8.1           4.35
      .......................................................
      (Overall Grand Mean = (18.9+8.1+4.35)/3 = 10.45 min)


    STEP 1 — Calculate GROUP MEANS:
      Mean_A = (18.2+19.5+17.8+20.1) / 4 = 75.6/4 = 18.9
      Mean_B = (8.1+7.8+8.5+8.0) / 4 = 32.4/4 = 8.1
      Mean_C = (4.2+4.8+3.9+4.5) / 4 = 17.4/4 = 4.35

    STEP 2 — Grand Mean:
      Grand Mean = (75.6+32.4+17.4) / 12 = 125.4/12 = 10.45

    STEP 3 — SSB (Sum of Squares Between groups):
      SSB = n × Σ(Group Mean - Grand Mean)²
            where n = replications per group (4 here)

      SSB = 4 × [(18.9-10.45)² + (8.1-10.45)² + (4.35-10.45)²]
          = 4 × [(8.45)² + (-2.35)² + (-6.10)²]
          = 4 × [71.40 + 5.52 + 37.21]
          = 4 × 114.13
          = 456.52

    STEP 4 — SSW (Sum of Squares Within groups):
      SSW = Σ(each value - its group mean)²

      Group A:
        (18.2-18.9)² = 0.49
        (19.5-18.9)² = 0.36
        (17.8-18.9)² = 1.21
        (20.1-18.9)² = 1.44   Sum_A = 3.50

      Group B:
        (8.1-8.1)²  = 0.00
        (7.8-8.1)²  = 0.09
        (8.5-8.1)²  = 0.16
        (8.0-8.1)²  = 0.01   Sum_B = 0.26

      Group C:
        (4.2-4.35)² = 0.0225
        (4.8-4.35)² = 0.2025
        (3.9-4.35)² = 0.2025
        (4.5-4.35)² = 0.0225  Sum_C = 0.45

      SSW = 3.50 + 0.26 + 0.45 = 4.21

    STEP 5 — Degrees of Freedom:
      df_Between = k - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2
                   (k = number of groups)
      df_Within  = N - k = 12 - 3 = 9
                   (N = total observations)

    STEP 6 — Mean Squares:
      MSB = SSB / df_Between = 456.52 / 2 = 228.26
      MSW = SSW / df_Within  = 4.21 / 9   = 0.468

    STEP 7 — F-Statistic:
      F = MSB / MSW = 228.26 / 0.468 = 487.7

    STEP 8 — ANOVA TABLE:
      .......................................................
      Source       SS       df    MS        F
      .......................................................
      Between      456.52   2     228.26    487.7
      (Strategies)
      Within        4.21    9       0.47
      (Error)
      Total        460.73   11
      .......................................................

    STEP 9 — Decision:
      F_critical (df1=2, df2=9, alpha=0.05) = 4.26 (from table)
      F_calculated = 487.7 >> 4.26

      487.7 > 4.26 → REJECT H0
      → The three strategies produce SIGNIFICANTLY DIFFERENT
        waiting times. The differences are NOT due to chance.
      → Choosing the number of servers MATTERS. ✓

      In plain English:
      "Adding servers significantly reduces waiting time.
       This is a real effect, not just random variation."


  ....................................
  C. POST-HOC TESTS (after ANOVA)
  ....................................

    . ANOVA tells you: "At least one group is different."
    . But it does NOT tell you: "WHICH groups are different."
    . Post-hoc tests (like Tukey's HSD) compare pairs:
      Is A different from B? Is B different from C? Etc.

    . In our example:
      All three strategies are clearly different from each other.
      But in cases where groups are closer together,
      post-hoc tests determine which specific pairs differ.


  ....................................
  D. TWO-WAY ANOVA
  ....................................

    . Two factors being varied simultaneously.
    . Tests main effect of Factor 1, main effect of Factor 2,
      AND the INTERACTION between them.

    . SCENARIO:
      Factor 1: Number of servers (1 or 2)
      Factor 2: Queue discipline (FIFO or Priority)
      Output:   Average waiting time

      Two-way ANOVA checks:
        . Does number of servers affect wait time? (Main effect 1)
        . Does queue discipline affect wait time? (Main effect 2)
        . Does the COMBINATION matter differently?
          (Interaction effect)

    . Interaction example:
      Priority queue with 1 server reduces wait for VIP customers.
      With 2 servers, priority matters less (everyone gets served fast).
      This is an INTERACTION EFFECT.

  KEY FORMULAS REFERENCE
============================================================

  REGRESSION:
  . Y = a + b.X
  . b = [n.SUM(XY) - SUM(X).SUM(Y)] / [n.SUM(X²) - (SUM(X))²]
  . a = [SUM(Y) - b.SUM(X)] / n
  . R² = 1 - SSresidual / SStotal

  ANOVA:
  . SSB = n × SUM(Group_Mean - Grand_Mean)²
  . SSW = SUM SUM (each_value - its_group_mean)²
  . SST = SSB + SSW
  . MSB = SSB / (k-1)
  . MSW = SSW / (N-k)
  . F   = MSB / MSW

  DECISION:
  . If F_calculated > F_critical → Reject H0 (groups differ)
  . If F_calculated < F_critical → Fail to reject H0 (no diff)
